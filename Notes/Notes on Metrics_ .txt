Gemini generated and fable refined metrics: 

A paper with eight metrics validated against a few hundred human-rated poems, plus a released benchmark, beats a paper with twenty unvalidated formulas. So treat the list below as a menu, build in order, and stop adding once you have a validated core.
The list is ordered so that each tier reuses machinery from the previous one. Every metric gets: what it measures, how to compute it, what you need.
Tier 1: sound and surface. Pure rules and counting, no machine learning, no annotation.
1. Alliteration density (anuprāsa): repetition of consonants beyond what chance predicts. Convert text to phonemes, group similar consonants into classes (k/kh/g/gh together, etc.), count each class per line, and score the deviation from expected counts (z-score against corpus frequencies). The baseline matters: frequent consonants like t/n/r repeat naturally, and raw counts reward that noise.
2. Rhyme and caesura compliance (prāsa, yati): fixed-position sound rules. Prāsa: the consonant of the second syllable must match across lines. Yati: the sound at a position prescribed by the meter must belong to the same permitted sound-class as the line-initial sound. Both are lookup-table checks once you have a syllable splitter and the class tables from prosody manuals. Output pass/fail or a graded score.
3. Euphony index (mādhurya, "sweetness"): proportion of soft sounds: nasals, y/r/l/v, unaspirated stops, few consonant clusters. A weighted phoneme score averaged over the poem.
4. Density index (ojas, "vigor"): long compounds, heavy conjunct clusters, aspirated stops. Compute average compound length and clusters per syllable.
5. Clarity index (prasāda):[a] how easily the text parses. Proxies: average word frequency, and perplexity under a language model (LM), meaning how surprised a trained model is by the text; lower surprise = plainer text.
6. Defect penalties (doṣa): the negative side. Detect cacophony (harsh clusters with no expressive purpose), accidental repetition (same word or sound repeated outside the sanctioned rhyme/alliteration positions from metrics 1 and 2), and obscurity (no valid word segmentation exists). Without penalties, any composite used as a generation reward degenerates into tongue-twisters.
7. Rhythm profile (laya): gradient metrical quality instead of binary pass/fail. Represent each line as a heavy/light syllable string (guru/laghu), compute edit distance to the target meter pattern for partial credit, and compute regularity statistics (autocorrelation, entropy rate) of that string for rhythmic texture.
Tier 2: needs a language model and a dictionary, still no human annotation.
8. Segmentation pun score (śabda-śleṣa): text that splits into words in two different ways, both meaningful, possible because sandhi fuses words at the surface. Run a segmenter that returns all valid splits, score each split's coherence with the rest of the verse using an LM, and only if two or more splits survive the coherence filter, score how balanced they are (entropy or top-2 margin). The filter is the real metric; without it you measure garbage ambiguity, since most alternative splits of any long compound are nonsense.
9. Double-meaning score (artha-śleṣa): one word whose two dictionary senses both fit the context. For each word, embed each of its sense definitions and embed the surrounding verse; a word scores high when its second-best-fitting sense still fits well and the two senses are semantically distant from each other. Needs a sense dictionary plus a sentence embedder.
10. Obliquity profile (vakrokti, "crooked speech": the theory that poetry is deliberate deviation from plain expression at several levels: sound, word choice, grammar, sentence, and beyond). Score the text with two LMs, one trained on prose and one on poetry: surprisal under the prose model minus surprisal under the poetry model, at character, word, and construction granularity. High values mean deviation from prose that the poetic tradition licenses, as opposed to plain error, which both models find surprising.
11. Suggestion density (dhvani: implied meaning, ranked by the tradition above stated meaning and above mere ornamentation). Produce the literal gloss of the verse, elicit interpretations (annotators or a large model), then count interpretations readers consistently draw that are not logically entailed by the gloss. Entailment is checked with natural language inference (NLI) models: entailed = stated, neutral-but-consistently-inferred = suggested. The suggested-to-stated ratio is the score.
12. Meaning-access curve (pāka: the grape/banana/coconut scale of classical criticism, where a grape poem yields its meaning immediately and a coconut poem only after cracking the shell). Read the poem incrementally and measure at each point how settled the interpretation is (LM uncertainty over continuations, or drift of a running-summary embedding). The curve's shape classifies early-payoff vs late-payoff poems.[b]
13. Image novelty (kavi-samaya: the stock conventions poets share, like face-compared-to-moon). Build an inventory of conventional source→target comparison pairs from corpora and manuals; score each detected comparison by its distance from that inventory. Near zero = cliché, far = invention (or incoherence, which metric 6 catches).
Tier 3: needs human annotation or genuinely open modeling.
14. Simile extraction (upamā): detect similes and fill four roles: the thing described, the thing it's compared to, the shared property, and the marker word ("like/as" equivalents). Marker words make candidate detection easy; the hard part is inferring the shared property when the poet leaves it implicit. Full vs elided simile types fall out as which roles are present. Needs a small annotated set.
15. Metaphor detection (rūpaka: identification without a marker, "face-moon"): flag pairs with low literal similarity but high contextual compatibility. Standard metaphor-detection setup; thresholds must be fit on data, not assumed.
16. Poetic justification (kāvyaliṅga: a reason stated in support of a poetic claim): score the strength of the claim-given-reason link with a generative LM, since classical-register NLI models don't exist.
17. Emotion arc (rasa: the eight or nine canonical aesthetic emotions: erotic, heroic, pathetic, comic, furious, fearful, disgusting, marvelous, peaceful). Classify each stanza, then score dominance (one ruling emotion = the classical ideal), penalize traditionally prohibited clashes, and track the trajectory across the poem. Needs annotation.
18. Propriety score (aucitya: everything should suit everything: meter suits mood, diction suits register). Learn compatibility matrices (meter↔emotion, vocabulary-register consistency) from a corpus and score incongruence.
19. Figure blending (saṅsṛṣṭi = independent figures coexisting; saṅkara = figures fused so that each depends on the other). Once detectors 1, 8, 9, 14, 15 emit labeled text spans, check span overlap and mutual dependency; overlapping and interdependent = saṅkara. Purely derivative of earlier metrics, which is why it's last among detectors.
20. Composite score, learned not hand-set: collect human ratings or pairwise preferences on a few hundred poems, fit the weights by regression or a Bradley-Terry preference model, and report each metric's individual correlation with human judgment. The score vector is the primary output; the single number exists only for ranking.
Suggested path: 1→7 in order (each is days of work), then 8 and 10 as the first LM-dependent ones, then 11, which is the most novel single contribution in the whole list. Tell me a number and I'll spec that metric fully: exact inputs, algorithm, edge cases, and evaluation.

Other languages and their metrics : 

Yes, there's a substantial body of work, and it splits cleanly into five clusters. The short version relevant to you: meter-compliance metrics exist for many languages and are essentially a solved problem class; sound-device metrics exist in scattered form; quality-validation studies exist but are rare; and almost nobody has built the figure-of-speech/semantic layer. That last gap is where an alaṅkāra-based system would sit.
Cluster 1: scansion and meter identification (form compliance). The most developed cluster. English tools include Scandroid (Hartman) and ZeuScansion (Agirrezabal et al.), with machine-learning scansion trained against human-annotated corpora (Agirrezabal, Alegria, Hulden, COLING 2016). Machine-learning metrical annotation now covers Middle High German (Estes and Hench 2016), Spanish (Navarro-Colorado; de la Rosa's Rantanplan in PoetryLab), Middle Dutch syllabification and stress (Haverals), and German/English metrical tagging (Haider 2021), mostly via bidirectional recurrent networks with conditional random field output layers. For quantitative (weight-based) meters like yours: classical Greek hexameter has rule-based scansion via weighted finite-state transducers, and Sanskrit has meter identifiers (Melnad/Scharf's work, Rajagopalan's chandas tool, Terdalkar's Chandojnanam), which are the nearest neighbors to your trie/CFG validator.
Cluster 2: the closest structural analog is Arabic ʿarūḍ. Like chandas, it's a closed system of canonical quantitative meters over a non-Latin script. Yousef et al. built character-level RNN classifiers over 1.5M+ verses, hitting 96.4% across the 16 Arabic meters, and Al-shaibani, Alyafeai and Ahmad (Pattern Recognition Letters 2020) reached 94.3% on 14 meters with a purpose-built benchmark dataset, explicitly bypassing the rule-based Arudi encoding that traditional classification required. Their rules-vs-end-to-end tension is exactly your deterministic-scorer-vs-neural question, and their conclusion is instructive: meter ID saturates quickly, so the interesting work moves elsewhere.
Cluster 3: sound-device metrics beyond meter. Rhyme density from the rap-generation literature (Malmi et al., DopeLearning, KDD 2016: longest matching vowel sequences per word) and Hirjee & Brown's probabilistic imperfect-rhyme detection, which scores phoneme pairs with a similarity matrix learned from real rhyming data, the way BLOSUM matrices score amino acids. That second one is the single most adaptable technique for you: prāsa-maitri and yati equivalence classes are literally a hand-built version of such a matrix, and you could also learn one from your corpus and compare it against the classical tables, which is a paper-worthy result on its own. Alliteration and rhyme classifiers also ship as evaluation tools with style-conditioned generators (ByGPT5, Belouadi & Eger 2023, English/German).
Cluster 4: quality validation, the rare and valuable kind. Kao & Jurafsky compared stylistic and content features of award-winning versus amateur poets: diction, sound devices, emotive language, imagery, and found concreteness of imagery to be the strongest computable indicator of professional-quality poetry. This is the design your validation needs: features → acclaimed-vs-ordinary discrimination. For the rasa analog, PO-EMO (Haider et al., LREC 2020) annotated German and English poetry line-by-line with aesthetic emotions as elicited in the reader rather than expressed in the text, allowing multiple labels per line for mixed emotions, reaching κ=.70 expert agreement, with BERT classification proving hard (.52 F1). Their annotation protocol (reader-response framing, multi-label, expert-vs-crowd comparison) is a ready-made template for rasa annotation, and they survey prior Indian-language poetry emotion corpora, including an Odia corpus (Mohanty et al. 2018) and a multi-language Indian corpus annotated with Ekman's categories plus Love and Courage (Sreeja & Mahalakshmi 2019), so a lineage to cite exists.
Cluster 5: theory and the statistical-versification tradition. Hanson & Kiparsky's parametric theory of meter (Language, 1996) and Fabb & Halle's grid-based theory (2008) are the cross-linguistic formalisms; framing chandas in their parameter space is how you make a Telugu system legible to general reviewers. The Russian/Czech statistical tradition (Gasparov; Plecháč's annotated Czech verse corpora and rhyme detection) established meter statistics as evidence for stylistics and authorship. Kevin Ryan's work on gradient syllable weight, including in Sanskrit meter, shows that even "binary" guru/laghu has statistical fine structure, which supports the laya-beyond-scansion metric from the earlier list.
Relevance, stated bluntly. Three transfers and one warning.
 Transfer 1: Arabic proves the meter-classification layer is commoditized, so a paper whose contribution is meter ID will not clear a top venue; your contribution has to be the layers above it. 
Transfer 2: Hirjee-Brown-style learned phoneme-similarity matrices give you graded prāsa/yati/anuprāsa scoring for free methodologically.
Transfer 3: Kao-Jurafsky's professional-vs-amateur design and PO-EMO's annotation protocol are the two validation blueprints worth copying. The warning: stress-based scansion approaches, the bulk of English and Spanish work, are explicitly not applicable to quantitative meter, so tool reuse from those languages is near zero even though the study designs transfer fully.
If you read one study: the recent survey "Understanding poetry using natural language processing tools" (Digital Scholarship in the Humanities, 2024), which maps the whole tool landscape and hands you a related-work section. If you read one empirical paper, Kao & Jurafsky 2012 (the workshop version is 10 pages and free on the ACL Anthology). If you read one closest-language paper, Al-shaibani et al. 2020 on Arabic.
